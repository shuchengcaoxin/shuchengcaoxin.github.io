MetaboCausal · Evidence agents, part 2

Building an AI agent that can’t fake drug-target evidencefrom a metabolite in blood, through the gene behind it, to whether it is worth a drug

LLMs hallucinate, and this is the second agent I built to stop one from doing it. This one starts from a molecule in blood (a metabolite) rather than a protein, so before it can say anything about a drug target it has to find the gene behind that molecule. The verdict itself is computed by a function, not written by a model.

By Shucheng Cao (Bangli Cao) with the MetaboCausal team · 12 September 2026 · 6 min read

This is the second agent I have built this way. The first one answers protein–disease questions and is where the recording-and-rendering core comes from. That core carried over unchanged. Almost everything else is new, because a metabolite is a harder place to start than a protein: its identity has to be resolved first, and the gene acting behind it has to be nominated before there is a target question at all. On top of that sit a literature layer, a biochemical-context module, and a classifier that decides the verdict so that no model writes it. If none of this biology is yours, that section is the one to read.

Background

Think of the body as a production line. DNA is the blueprint, RNA the work order, a protein is the machine, and a metabolite is the finished goods on the shelf — glucose, LDL cholesterol, creatinine, the numbers on an ordinary blood test. How you feel depends on what is on the shelf. Diet, medication and gut bacteria put things on it directly, without passing through the genes at all, so a metabolite is the one reading that sums up both what you inherited and what happened to you, and it sits one step from the disease.

But you cannot make a drug out of the shelf. A statin lowers cholesterol by blocking HMGCR, the enzyme that makes it — an enzyme being a protein whose job is to build or break one metabolite. So a question that starts at a metabolite still has to end at a gene, and finding that gene is the step this agent had to add.

A treatment can improve a number in a blood test and still fail to help patients. That gap matters to scientists choosing experiments, and to drug teams deciding where to spend years of work.

Useful evidence is scattered across genetic studies, papers, drug records and clinical trials. Each source tells only part of the story. Reading them separately makes it easy to miss a warning—or mistake a promising marker for a useful treatment.

Our question: can we trace a path from a molecule in blood, through genetics and biology, to what happened in the clinic?

A failure worth tracing

The story began before Phase 3. In 1989, an inherited CETP change ↗ was linked to much higher HDL. Target-level experiments in rabbits then reduced atherosclerotic lesions in 1998 ↗, and another CETP inhibitor showed benefit in 2000 ↗. These findings supported the biology, but they did not establish that torcetrapib itself would be safe or help patients.

Torcetrapib was designed to block CETP and raise HDL cholesterol, often called “good cholesterol.” A Phase 1 study published in 2004 ↗ showed that the blood marker moved in the expected direction. But the large Phase 3 trial was stopped ↗ after more deaths and cardiovascular events in the treatment group.

Later genetic studies helped separate two questions. One suggested that the rise in blood pressure ↗ was probably a drug-specific side effect. Another challenged the assumption ↗ that raising HDL alone would automatically prevent heart attacks. A different CETP drug later reduced coronary events ↗. The lesson is more useful than a simple “target failed” label: the marker, the biological target and the drug itself are related, but they are not interchangeable.

What the agent does

It is for people who have a molecule in blood and a disease in mind and want to know whether the two are connected: someone holding a biobank result they cannot yet explain, someone in pharma R&D deciding what to chase next, or anyone curious about the link.

You ask it in Slack. Type @MetaboCausal, a metabolite and a disease, and the answer comes back in the thread. There is nothing to install and no page to learn — the point of putting it there is that asking should cost no more than typing a message to a colleague.

1 · AskOne molecule, one disease.
2 · TraceGenetic results, the genes behind the molecule, drug history.
3 · DecideWhat supports the idea, what conflicts, what is missing.

Behind that it reads public sources: EpiGraphDB for causal estimates other people have published, OpenGWAS for whether the genetic instruments exist, the GWAS Catalog for association background, Open Targets, ChEMBL and gnomAD for the genes, plus Europe PMC and Semantic Scholar for the literature. A model can explain the result in plain language afterwards, but the sources, numbers and dates stay on the page for checking.

What it ships

The first thing it does is tell you what it thinks you meant. Ask about “HDL cholesterol” and it comes back as high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, CHEBI:47775, with the four spellings it will accept; “coronary artery disease” comes back as coronary artery disorder, MONDO:0005010. You see the substitution before anything is retrieved, because a wrong match makes every number under it answer a different question.

Then it asks which evidence you want, and waits. Not everyone needs all of it, and the slow parts say so: the GWAS Catalog arm adds about a hundred seconds, the gene arm five requests per gene.

What comes back is one card. For this pair it is State A: 48 published estimates carry an effect size, an uncertainty and a p-value, the smallest of them 1.08e-07. Retrieved colocalisation puts H4 at 1.000 for one pair of studies. Three genes are named as candidates behind the molecule — LIPG, ABCA1 and SCARB1 — each with its Open Targets score, its ChEMBL record and its gnomAD tolerance.

Two lines on the same card report failure. The instruments arm returned an error, so no hit count was retrieved, and it says so rather than printing a zero. Of nine matched outcome datasets only three came back. The footer records 19 ledger entries and the words retrieved, not computed.

Figures are one press each, and each one says what it is for: the direction and uncertainty of every retrieved estimate, how many strong instruments exist, or when the genetics spoke against when the clinic decided.

What carried over, what broke, and what I added

Three things carried over from the previous agent without change. Every tool call is recorded together with its return, word for word. The card is then built from those stored returns by code—tables, numbers, caveats, sources—so nothing a reader might want to check is written by a model. And whatever prose a model does add is compared against those same stored returns, and the run fails when the prose outruns them.

Two things had to be rebuilt, both because a metabolite is not a protein. Identity: a protein arrives with one accession, while a metabolite arrives as a common name, a synonym or a database id—so a run begins by resolving it through OLS4 and PubChem, and prints what it resolved to, because you cannot audit a resolution you cannot see. The effector gene: a protein is its own gene and a metabolite is not, so the gene has to be nominated—from an atlas or a paper where one exists, and otherwise from Human-GEM’s enzymes. That step is where a metabolite question becomes a target question, and everything downstream—druggability, population constraint, what the clinic already tried—hangs on which gene was named, so the nomination is printed together with the source it came from rather than quietly assumed.

The verdict is computed rather than written. A pure function reads the returns a run collected and assigns one of three states, and no model writes that sentence or can change it. In the two examples bundled with the public repository, urate × gout comes back State A—22 retrieved estimates, each carrying an effect size, an uncertainty and a p-value, retrieved from published sources and not computed here. Glycine × coronary artery disease comes back State B: no retrieved estimate exists, but the best exposure dataset carries 49 genome-wide significant hits and three outcome datasets are available, so that analysis is feasible and simply has not been run. State C is an evidence gap in the sources queried. None of the three, on its own, establishes causality.

Two decisions inside that classifier are the ones I would keep in any domain.

  • A later signal cannot rewrite an earlier judgement. Colocalisation runs after the state is assigned and its result is never handed back to the classifier, so nothing it returns can move a pair between states. It qualifies the evidence rather than quietly re-deciding it.
  • A failed request is not a negative answer. In the bundled glycine run, the GWAS Catalog call ran past its deadline with the response still arriving. The card prints that the request failed, says plainly that this is not the same statement as the Catalog holding no association for that trait, and the state does not move.

Two further layers sit on top of that core, and neither existed in the first agent. A literature layer widens the set of metabolite–disease pairs beyond the ones the MR resource already holds, through two independent searches whose overlapping results are matched and de-duplicated before anything moves downstream. A biochemical-context module built on Human-GEM nominates effector genes for metabolites where no atlas or paper names one, which is the case far more often than it is for proteins.

Both run in the working repository. The public snapshot linked above is the 12 September build and predates them, so its notes still describe those two as unfinished—worth saying plainly, because a reader who opens the repository should not have to reconcile it with this page on their own.

The interface is a Slack bot rather than one more standalone app, because the brief was agents that live where people already work. What the system does not do is held as firmly as what it does: it computes no MR and fits no colocalisation, and the public snapshot ships agent.py as an unfinished template rather than a finished model loop.

How the product is improving

We split the work into three MVPs—small, usable versions that each add one clear capability.

MVP1 · Working core Make one result understandable

Turn reviewed molecule–disease pairs into source-linked evidence cards, with browser and Slack output.

MVP2 · Broader coverage Find more useful questions

Expand the starting table from large studies and two independent literature searches, then match and remove repeated results before they move downstream.

MVP3 · Deeper context Connect the result to biology and development

Add gene, pathway, drug and clinical context, including Human-GEM, and rerun the same pipeline on the combined evidence.

This staged plan gives managers a visible product at every step, while letting technical contributors test each layer before the next one is added.

What the technical design protects

  • Traceability: dates, numbers and claims point back to sources.
  • Separation: retrieval builds the evidence record; the AI model explains it.
  • Challenge testing: in one Slack test, a teammate supplied a deliberately false premise. The system rejected the unsupported claim instead of inventing evidence. This passed test shows the safeguard working in that case; it is not a claim that any AI can never hallucinate.
  • Reproducibility: the same structured pipeline can run again when new pairs arrive.
  • Practical access: OpenAI and Claude are supported now, with user-provided model keys planned.

The public description stays at the product level. The team’s prompts, matching rules, internal data structure and deployment details remain in the private working repository.

The public demo repository ↗ holds the runnable part. It regenerates two example cards from a bundled snapshot of earlier API responses, needing no account, key or Slack install—a historical replay rather than a live query. The repository also states which modules are unfinished, so a reader can separate what runs today from what is planned.

What it does not claim: MetaboCausal does not prove that a drug will work. It helps a team see the available evidence—and the gaps—before choosing the next test.

Related work and what comes next

MetaboCausal is a separate hackathon project related to OpenCausal ↗, my earlier work on source-linked evidence cards for proteins and diseases. Here, the starting point is a molecule in blood.

The literature layer and the biochemical-context module are built; the next step is to publish them in the open snapshot, so that the public repository matches what actually runs. The longer-term goal is unchanged: a tool that lets a team bring their own data and their own model key, while every conclusion stays tied to evidence they can inspect.
