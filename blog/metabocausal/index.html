When a better blood marker is not a better medicine

MetaboCausal connects genetic evidence to disease and drug-development history, helping teams decide what to test next.

By Shucheng Cao (Bangli Cao) with the MetaboCausal team · 12 September 2026 · 4 min read

The problem

A treatment can improve a number in a blood test and still fail to help patients. That gap matters to scientists choosing experiments, and to drug teams deciding where to spend years of work.

Useful evidence is scattered across genetic studies, papers, drug records and clinical trials. Each source tells only part of the story. Reading them separately makes it easy to miss a warning—or mistake a promising marker for a useful treatment.

Our question: can we trace a path from a molecule in blood, through genetics and biology, to what happened in the clinic?

A failure worth tracing

Torcetrapib was designed to block CETP and raise HDL cholesterol, often called “good cholesterol.” The blood marker moved in the expected direction. But the large trial was stopped ↗ after more deaths and cardiovascular events in the treatment group.

Later genetic studies helped separate two questions. One suggested that the rise in blood pressure ↗ was probably a drug-specific side effect. Another challenged the assumption ↗ that raising HDL alone would automatically prevent heart attacks. A different CETP drug later reduced coronary events ↗. The lesson is more useful than a simple “target failed” label: the marker, the biological target and the drug itself are related, but they are not interchangeable.

What MetaboCausal does

A user starts with a small molecule measured in blood and a disease. MetaboCausal then builds a compact evidence trail:

1 · AskChoose one molecule and one disease.
2 · TraceConnect genetic results, possible genes and drug history.
3 · DecideSee what supports the idea, what conflicts and what is missing.

The system uses public research sources, including genetic databases, Europe PMC, Semantic Scholar, Open Targets and clinical-trial records. An AI model can explain the result in plain language, but the sources, numbers and dates remain visible for checking. The current integration supports OpenAI and Claude.

What exists today

For the hackathon, we built a working evidence-card pipeline, a browser demo and Slack-ready output. The first version starts from an existing table of molecule–disease results. We are now widening that evidence base through two independent literature searches, adding gene and pathway context, and turning the clinical timeline into a reusable step.

What it does not claim: MetaboCausal does not prove that a drug will work. It helps a team see the available evidence—and the gaps—before choosing the next test.

Related work and what comes next

MetaboCausal is a separate hackathon project related to OpenCausal ↗, my earlier work on source-linked evidence cards for proteins and diseases. Here, the starting point is a molecule in blood.

Next we will merge the wider literature results, add pathway context, run the pipeline again on the combined table, and record a two-minute demo. The longer-term goal is a secure tool that lets teams bring their own data and model key while keeping every conclusion tied to evidence they can inspect.
